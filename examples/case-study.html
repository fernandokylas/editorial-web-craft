Marrick Fabrication Ltd — Precision engineering

Releasing £14.6m of cash from a business that believed it had none to spare.

A fourteen-week working-capital engagement for a three-plant metal fabricator took its cash conversion cycle from 93 to 55 days without a headcount change, a new system, or a single supplier walking away.

01 — Context

Jan 2026 baseline

Profitable on paper, tight at the bank

Marrick makes precision-fabricated enclosures and chassis for rail, defence and medical-equipment customers from plants in Rotherham, Telford and Kilmarnock. Operating margin had held between 9 % and 11 % for four years. Cash had not: the revolving credit facility was 84 % drawn by January, inventory had grown 31 % in two years against 12 % revenue growth, and receivables had crept from 44 to 58 days.

The trigger was a refinancing due in Q4 2026. The lender had signalled that headroom, not profit, would set the terms. The board asked Halden & Roe for a view on how much cash was recoverable from the balance sheet before it renegotiated, and whether recovering it would damage the customer and supplier relationships the business depends on.

Two earlier internal cash programmes had produced a temporary dip in receivables and a permanent rise in the number of people chasing them. Nobody at Marrick wanted a third.

02 — Approach

Weeks 1–4 diagnostic

Weeks 5–14 delivery

Change the defaults, not the people

The diagnostic ran on 26 months of receivables, payables and inventory ledgers at transaction level, 41 structured interviews across finance, sales, planning and the three plants, and a movement analysis of all 12,400 stocked SKUs. We were looking for the rules and system defaults that quietly decided when cash moved, rather than for people who were slow.

Delivery was organised around three workstreams, each owned by a Marrick manager with one of ours alongside:

  • Receivables. Invoice accuracy at the point of dispatch, credit-term hygiene, and a credit-hold rule that the sales director could live with.
  • Inventory. Safety-stock parameters, minimum order quantities, and a disposition route for stock that had stopped moving.
  • Payables. Harmonised supplier terms with an early-payment option, so that stretching terms never became a way of borrowing from small suppliers.

Every change was tested on one plant for two weeks before the other two adopted it. Where a change moved a number in the wrong direction, it was reversed and written up rather than defended.

Sixty-one percent of overdue receivables traced back to invoices that were wrong when they left the building: a purchase-order number that did not match, a missing delivery note, a price from a superseded quote. Customers were not slow payers; they were being handed reasons to query.

Inventory told a similar story. 2,300 SKUs, £3.1m at cost, had not moved in eighteen months; most were safety stock for parts whose customers had re-specified years earlier, still reordered because nobody had switched the parameter off. On the payables side, 340 suppliers were being paid on seventeen different term structures, several of them shorter than the ones Marrick's own customers enjoyed.

None of this required judgement to find. It required someone to look at the settings.

04 — Recommendations

Adopted by the board

21 May 2026

Six changes that hold without a programme office

Each recommendation was accepted in full. The first four were live before the engagement closed; the last two took effect from the new financial year.

  1. Invoice at dispatch, with a three-way match

    No invoice leaves without a matched purchase order, delivery note and current price. Mismatches are queued to the account manager the same day, not discovered in a dunning call six weeks later.

  2. A credit-hold rule that sales agreed to

    Accounts over 15 days past terms go on hold automatically; the sales director can release a hold once per account per quarter. The rule is public inside the business, so no one is surprised.

  3. Quarterly slow-mover disposition

    Anything without movement for twelve months is reviewed by planning, sales and finance together, and either sold, reworked, returned or written down. The committee's authority is capped so it cannot become a way of hiding losses.

  4. Supplier terms harmonised to 45 days

    One standard, with a 2 % discount for payment at 15 days that small suppliers can elect into. Marrick pays later on average and its smallest suppliers get paid sooner than before.

  5. A weekly cash cockpit for the CFO

    One page: the three cycle metrics, the revolver position and the ten largest overdue accounts. Reviewed on Monday mornings with the plant general managers on the call.

  6. Cash in the plant bonus

    A quarter of each general manager's variable pay now depends on their plant's inventory days, alongside the existing output and quality measures. What gets paid for gets watched.

Key takeaway

For boards and CFOs

Working capital is a design problem, not a discipline problem

Every pound was sitting behind a setting that someone had chosen once and nobody had revisited.

The three cash programmes Marrick had run before this one all assumed the fix was effort: more calls, tighter chasing, a monthly stock count. Effort decays. A default does not. The £14.6m came from changing about forty parameters, rules and templates and then leaving them alone, which is why it was still there at the September month-end.

If your business is profitable and short of cash, the money is almost certainly in the settings rather than in the people.

Questions

Asked by every board we meet

Frequently asked

How quickly does cash start to move?

Receivables respond first, usually within one billing cycle of fixing invoice accuracy, because customers stop having reasons to hold payment. Inventory takes longest; safety-stock changes only show up as the old stock is consumed, so the full effect arrives over two to three quarters. At Marrick, 60 % of the total was visible by week 10.

Does this need new software?

Almost never. Every ERP we have worked in already has the credit-hold, three-way-match and reorder-parameter features that matter; they are switched off or set to values chosen at go-live. The cash cockpit at Marrick is a spreadsheet fed by a scheduled export. We would rather change forty settings in a system people already use than introduce a forty-first system.

How do you charge, and what does it cost against the cash released?

A fixed fee for the four-week diagnostic, quoted before we start, which ends with a sized and sequenced plan you are free to run yourself. Delivery is a fixed weekly rate for the team on site. We do not take a share of the cash released; it makes advisers argue for the largest number rather than the one that holds. On this engagement the total fee was under 4 % of the cash released in the first year.
