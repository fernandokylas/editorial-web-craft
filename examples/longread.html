The Quiet Ledger

How mid-market CFOs are rethinking cash — and why the most consequential shift in corporate finance since the rate shock is happening in a spreadsheet nobody presents to the board.

By Ingrid Halvorsen · 17 September 2026 · 14 min read

01

01 — The shock

Mar 2022 – Jul 2023

+525 bp

US policy rate, cumulative

The cash that wasn't there

For a decade, the finance function of a $300 million manufacturer could afford to be relaxed about cash. Revolving credit was cheap, covenants were loose, and a receivable that took 65 days to settle instead of 45 cost, in interest terms, almost nothing. The balance sheet was managed at the quarter; cash was managed by the bank.

That ended in fourteen months. Between March 2022 and July 2023 the US policy rate rose 525 basis points; term loans repriced within a quarter, and revolvers that had drawn at 3 % were suddenly drawing at 8.5 %. For a company carrying $60 million of floating-rate debt, the annual interest bill rose by more than $3 million — roughly the operating profit of a business unit.

What the rate shock exposed was not leverage but inattention. In Meridian's survey of 412 mid-market finance leaders (revenue $50 million to $1 billion), 61 per cent said that in 2021 nobody in their organisation owned a rolling cash forecast. By mid-2026 that figure had fallen to 9 per cent. The work of reconstruction — quiet, unglamorous and largely invisible from outside — is the subject of this piece.

02

02 — The instrument

Direct method, weekly

13 wks

Forecast horizon

Thirteen weeks, every Monday

The instrument at the centre of the change is old. The 13-week direct cash flow forecast — receipts and disbursements, by week, by bank account, reconciled to actuals every Monday morning — was a restructuring tool, deployed by turnaround advisers when a company was already in trouble. Its association with distress is exactly why healthy companies avoided it.

That stigma has gone. “We run it in the good years now because it’s the only view of the business that doesn’t lie,” says Marcus Adebayo, chief financial officer of Halden Fabrication, a $410 million industrial group based in Ohio. “The P&L can be argued with. Accruals can be argued with. The bank balance on Friday afternoon cannot.”

The discipline is in the reconciliation, not the forecast. Week one is compared with what actually happened; every variance is explained line by line; the next twelve weeks are re-forecast in the light of it. Done properly, it forces sales, operations and procurement into the same room each week to answer a single question: where is the cash going to come from, and when?

For CFOs who inherited a treasury function that lived inside the controller's month-end close, that weekly cadence has been the larger cultural shift. Cash, once a residual — the number left over after everything else had been accounted for — has become a managed input.

03 — The metrics

412 companies, FY23 – FY26

−11 days

Median cash conversion cycle change

Days, not dollars

The vocabulary changed too. Working-capital conversations that used to be conducted in dollars — how much is tied up in stock, how large is the receivables book — are now conducted in days: days sales outstanding, days payable outstanding, days inventory outstanding, and the net figure, the cash conversion cycle. Days are comparable across business units and across competitors; dollars are not.

The pattern across sectors is consistent: receivables collected faster, payables stretched where suppliers would tolerate it, and inventory brought down hardest in distribution, where the post-pandemic overstock had been most severe. The one sector that moved the other way — software — was responding to a different pressure: customers demanding monthly rather than annual billing, which lengthened DSO even as everything else tightened.

How the ledger was built

Companies were drawn from public filings and audited private accounts with revenue between $50 million and $1 billion in fiscal 2023. Days metrics use year-end balances against annual revenue and cost of sales; software DIO is reported as zero where inventory is immaterial. Medians rather than means are used throughout, so a handful of large restructurings do not distort sector figures.

An eleven-day improvement in the median cash conversion cycle sounds modest. For a company with $500 million of revenue it is roughly $15 million of cash released permanently — capital that no longer needs to be borrowed, or that can be returned. Across the sample, Meridian estimates the change released $6.1 billion.

04

04 — The tooling

Primary forecast tool

38 %

Still a workbook

The treasury that lives in a spreadsheet

For all the talk of treasury-management platforms, the most common tool in the mid-market cash forecast is still a workbook. Thirty-eight per cent of respondents said their primary forecast lived in Excel; a further 24 per cent used a spreadsheet that pulled from an ERP but was maintained by hand. The reasons are practical rather than nostalgic: the forecast has to be rebuilt every week, and the people rebuilding it need to see every assumption.

What has changed is what feeds the workbook. Bank connectivity — once a treasury-only luxury requiring a SWIFT membership and a six-figure integration — is now available through API aggregators for a few thousand dollars a year. Balances that used to be keyed in from a portal each morning arrive automatically, reconciled, before the treasury analyst has finished a coffee. The spreadsheet remains; the typing has gone.

The cultural effect is easy to underestimate. When the opening balance is trusted, argument moves to the assumptions: will the Meyer contract pay in week six or week eight? Is the inventory build for the fourth quarter really necessary? Those are commercial questions, and in the better-run companies Meridian spoke to they are now answered by the commercial team — in a weekly meeting that the CFO chairs but does not dominate. 05

05 — The consequence

Median net debt to EBITDA

2.1×

Down from 2.8× in 2023

What the quiet ledger changes

The visible results are on the balance sheet. Median net leverage across the sample fell from 2.8 times EBITDA in 2023 to 2.1 times in 2026, and only part of that was earnings growth; most of it was cash. Covenant headroom widened. Several CFOs described renegotiating facilities from a position of strength for the first time in their careers.

The less visible result is a change in the CFO's relationship with the board. A board that sees a 13-week forecast every month, with last month's variance explained, stops asking whether the company can make payroll and starts asking what the cash should be used for. That is a capital-allocation conversation, and it is the conversation finance leaders say they always wanted to be having.

None of this required a new theory of the firm. The tools are decades old; the arithmetic is simple; the discipline is weekly. What the rate shock did was make the cost of not knowing visible — and once it was visible, the quiet ledger became the most important document in the building. It is unlikely to go back in the drawer when rates fall.
